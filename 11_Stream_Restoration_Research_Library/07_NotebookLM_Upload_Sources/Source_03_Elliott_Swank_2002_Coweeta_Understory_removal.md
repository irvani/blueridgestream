# NotebookLM Source 03: Forest Understory Hydrology & Water Yields (Coweeta Laboratory)

* **Full Title**: Long-term pattern of forest canopy cover and water yield after understory removal in the southern Appalachians
* **Authors**: Elliott, K.J. and Swank, W.T.
* **Published In**: *Forest Ecology and Management* (2002)
* **DOI**: [10.1016/S0378-1127(01)00481-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-1127(01)00481-2)

---

## 1. Executive Scientific Abstract
This publication examines the long-term hydrological and ecological consequences of removing the evergreen understory (dominated by *Rhododendron maximum* and mountain laurel *Kalmia latifolia*) in a southern Appalachian forest watershed at the **Coweeta Hydrologic Laboratory**. It monitors forest canopy response, soil moisture fluctuations, and watershed-scale streamflow yields for 15 years post-removal.

---

## 2. Experimental Methodology
* **Paired-Watershed Design**: Utilized two adjacent, high-gradient forested watersheds in Western North Carolina. Watershed 7 served as the treatment site, while Watershed 2 served as the untreated control.
* **Understory Removal**: All evergreen understory vegetation (rhododendron and laurel) was completely cut down in the treatment watershed, leaving the overstory hardwood trees (oak, hickory) intact.
* **Long-Term Monitoring**: Maintained continuous hydrological weirs to measure water yields and streamside soil moisture, combined with annual leaf area index (LAI) and canopy density surveys.

---

## 3. Core Scientific Findings
1. **Soil Moisture Control**: Proved that *Rhododendron maximum* acts as a dominant consumer of streamside soil moisture. The treatment watershed experienced an immediate, significant increase in soil moisture due to the removal of understory transpiration.
2. **Transient Streamflow Yields**: Understory removal caused a transient increase in streamflow water yields during the initial years post-cut, as less water was transpired by the evergreen understory.
3. **Hardwood Canopy Compensation**: The increase in water yield declined over the 15-year period. Overstory deciduous hardwood trees quickly capitalized on the freed-up soil moisture and nutrients, expanding their leaf canopies and compensating for the understory loss.

---

## 4. Applied Relevance to Blue Ridge Stream Restoration
* **Sourcing Argument**: Proves to private forestry and agricultural landowners that streamside *Rhododendron* thickets are powerful hydrologic regulators. Protecting or transplanting mature rhododendrons helps stabilize streamside soil moisture and manage upland stormwater runoff.
* **Vegetative Buffers**: Demonstrates the resilience of Southern Appalachian headwater forests and reinforces our UGA Warnell internship buffer planning models.
