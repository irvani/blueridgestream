Our Ecological Restoration Approach

Traditional hard civil engineering accelerates water flow, destroys habitats, and increases downstream erosion. We utilize Natural Channel Design (NCD) to restore streams back to their stable bankfull geometry.

🐟

Trout Life Ecology

Wild trout are highly sensitive indicator species requiring specific, coldwater conditions to survive and spawn:

  • Coldwater Habitats: Water temperatures must remain below 65°F (lethal threshold is 68°F).
  • High Oxygen: Turbulent rocky riffles aerate the water to supply >7 mg/L dissolved oxygen.
  • Clean Spawning Gravel: Sorted, silt-free gravel is essential for wild trout spawning nests ("redds").
🌧️

The Stormwater Runoff Threat

Rapid regional development and road erosion cause peak stormwater runoff that destroys old mountain streams:

  • Channel Overwidening: Extreme flows tear away banks, making channels wide, shallow, and warm.
  • Clay & Sand Siltation: Sediment washes down slopes, smothering cobbles and spawning beds.
  • EPT Food Chain Collapse: Mayflies and Caddisflies are obliterated, destroying the food web.
🌿

Natural Bioengineering

We use native wood, logs, root wads, and boulder cross-vanes to stabilize channels and trap sediment naturally:

  • Priority 1 Geomorphic Design: Narrowing channel bankfull widths to scour deep holding pools.
  • Instream Structures: Installing J-hooks and log vanes to redirect outer bank shear stress.
  • 100ft Canopy Buffers: Planting native Eastern Hemlocks to restore shade and lower water temperatures.

Connecting Fly Fishing Niche & Geomorphic Science

Hunter Morris's years guiding wild trout trips on Fly Fishing North Georgia revealed a painful truth: North Georgia’s legendary trout reaches are being destroyed by silt and heat. Here is how geomorphic stream restoration saves these waters.

Why Traditional Stream Work Fails Wild Trout

Standard contractors look at streams as drainage ditches. They pile up heavy granite rip-rap, straighten channels, and clear trees to control erosion. This is a disaster for trout. It speeds up current, raises temperatures, obliterates holding pools, and silts over critical gravel spawning beds.

Trout don't just need water—they need structural complexity. They need deep holding water under banks, cold thermoclines, swift food-producing riffles, and gravel nesting riffles (redds) that are clean of sand.

Commercial Mitigation · Pre-Application Stage

Noontootla Creek Corridor: Section C Anchor Reach

Located on a private holding on Noontootla Creek in the Upper Toccoa watershed (Fannin County), the Section C anchor reach is a perennial coldwater trout stream showing channel over-widening, chronic sediment loading, and bank instability. Our design intent is to restore it to a stable, gravel-dominated Rosgen C4 meander reconnected to its historic floodplain — and Section C anchors a potential corridor of 4+ miles of Noontootla Creek directly downstream of the Chattahoochee National Forest, which we are seeking to assemble with neighboring landowners under a Save Our Streams–led program.

The Section C baseline field survey was completed in July 2026 — 119 geotagged photographic frames with stadia-rod depth, bank-height, and sediment measurements — and the project has entered the USACE Savannah District pre-application process, targeting credit generation under the District's SOP for Calculating Compensatory Mitigation (v3.0) using the Georgia Stream Quantification Tool.

📡 Stream Telemetry Network · Sensor Boxes Coming Soon

Continuous Stream Data, From Sensor to Regulator

Annual monitoring reports are snapshots. We are developing solar-powered sensor nodes that record stream health around the clock — the continuous, auditable evidence that regulators, credit buyers, and landowners all want to see.

🌡️

Water Temperature

The line between thriving wild trout and an empty stream — tracked continuously against the coldwater thresholds trout need.

💨

Dissolved Oxygen

Round-the-clock oxygen readings show whether riffles are doing their job — and document ecological function for monitoring records.

📏

Stream Stage

Hydrostatic water-level sensing captures storm pulses and baseflow patterns that explain how the channel is actually behaving.

🌫️

Turbidity

Sediment is the number-one killer of spawning gravel. Turbidity spikes pinpoint when — and where — mud is entering the system.

How the Data Travels

  1. Solar sensor node in the stream — probes wired into a compact weatherproof enclosure.
  2. Long-range mesh radio (LoRa / Meshtastic) carries readings for miles across terrain with no cell coverage.
  3. A single uplink — one broadband or satellite connection serves an entire valley of nodes.
  4. Open data feeds — designed to share with USACE reviewers, Trout Unlimited's coldwater programs, researchers, and each landowner's phone.

Conventional gauge installations can run thousands of dollars per site. Our open-hardware nodes target a fraction of that cost — so the whole corridor gets instrumented, not just one point.

Status: v1 fully specified with vetted factory-direct suppliers — component sourcing is the next step. First field node planned for our Goldmine Hollow showcase reach; live corridor data coming soon.

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers on stream mitigation credits, landowner economics, and the USACE process in North Georgia.

What does stream restoration cost a landowner?

Nothing out of pocket. Save Our Streams funds the geomorphic survey, design, permitting, construction, plantings, and monitoring. If credits are approved by USACE and sold, you receive a 30% share of the proceeds.

What are stream mitigation credits?

Under Section 404 of the Clean Water Act, developers whose projects impact streams must compensate by purchasing mitigation credits. Credits are generated by restoring degraded streams under USACE oversight — in Georgia, per the Savannah District's Stream Mitigation SOP.

Who buys the credits?

Commercial developers, utilities, and infrastructure builders that need compensatory stream mitigation for USACE permits or Georgia EPD buffer variances within the same watershed service area.

When are credits released and sold?

In phases tied to USACE milestones — instrument or permit approval, construction completion, and demonstrated ecological performance during monitoring years. It is a multi-year process, and our continuous IoT stream monitoring is designed to support each release with hard data.

Will restoration improve the fishing on my property?

That is the point. Natural Channel Design creates deeper holding pools, colder water, cleaner spawning gravel, and stable banks — designed by a working North Georgia trout guide, not a drainage contractor.

Do I give up control of my land?

You keep ownership. The restored stream corridor is placed under a long-term protection mechanism (such as a conservation easement or deed restriction) so the ecological gains — and your credit value — are permanent. Terms are set out clearly in the landowner agreement before any work begins.

Is my stream eligible?

If you own land in North Georgia's trout watersheds (Toccoa, Coosa, Etowah, or Upper Chattahoochee basins) with roughly 500 feet or more of degraded, eroding perennial stream, you are a strong candidate. The field audit is free.

Where does the program stand today?

Our flagship Noontootla Creek reach in Fannin County has a completed baseline field survey, and a pre-application consultation with the USACE Savannah District Mitigation Branch was held in August 2026. Next steps are a site walkover and prospectus development under the District Stream SOP.

What does the first landowner document commit me to?

Only to letting us obtain a quote. The initial one-page authorization gives Save Our Streams a limited window (about six months) to develop a formal, government-reviewed quote for your stream — no construction obligation, no easement, and no cost. A separate final agreement, with your 30% credit share and protection terms spelled out, comes later and only if you choose to proceed.

How are stream mitigation credits calculated?

Credits are quantified under the USACE Savannah District's SOP for Calculating Compensatory Mitigation (v3.0, 2025) using the Georgia Stream Quantification Tool, which scores measurable functional lift in hydrology, hydraulics, geomorphology, physicochemical condition, and biology. Credits are released on the District's standard schedule as performance standards are met and verified through monitoring.
